# Why this project exists

> Markdown mirror of https://progress-tab.vercel.app/why. The origin story behind Progress Tab — a personal account of tracking meaningful progress and building lasting habits.

A few years ago, I realized something.

Social media, video games, and other forms of instant entertainment were enjoyable in the moment, but they rarely left anything behind. After winning a game, my life was exactly the same as before. A few months later, a new version would come out, and all those achievements would become meaningless.

Social media felt similar. I could spend hours reading, commenting, and following other people's lives, but when I closed the app, I had no new skills, no deeper understanding of the world, and no meaningful progress in my own life.

That led me to a simple question:

> What if I spent more time on activities that actually left something behind?

At first, that meant books and films. Not just entertainment, but works that could teach me something, challenge my perspective, or help me better understand the world around me.

Over time, I realized that I wanted more than knowledge for its own sake. I wanted to improve my life in a meaningful way. That required skills, opportunities, and resources. As a result, I became interested in topics like finance, self-education, and professional development.

Very quickly, I noticed that the deepest understanding often came from books. Articles, videos, and podcasts were useful, but books allowed me to explore subjects in far greater depth.

The problem was that I had no reading habit.

Reading felt slow. My attention would drift. I got tired quickly and often lost motivation to continue. Most importantly, the progress felt too small to matter.

So instead of asking myself how to read more, I started asking a different question:

> How can I avoid quitting?

I needed a simple system that would help me stay consistent, even on days when I had no motivation, energy, or desire to continue.

> The solution was surprisingly simple. I created a spreadsheet and started tracking how many pages I read each day. I gave myself only one rule: **read at least one page.**

One page sounds insignificant, but it was enough to get me started. And once I started, I often ended up reading much more than I had planned.

Weeks turned into months. The spreadsheet kept growing.

At the same time, my concentration improved. I became a better reader. Complex topics became easier to understand.

But the most important benefit appeared on the days when I wanted to quit.

Whenever I looked at the spreadsheet, I didn't just see numbers. I saw evidence of the work I had already put in. Months of small daily actions became visible.

That's when I learned something important.

> Instant entertainment provides immediate rewards. Meaningful activities work differently. They can genuinely improve your life, but the progress is often so gradual that it's difficult to notice from day to day.

When progress is invisible, it's easy to believe that nothing is happening. And when it feels like nothing is happening, it's easy to quit.

**Tracking changed that. It made slow progress visible.**

Over time, however, I discovered a flaw in my system. Measuring pages wasn't always fair. Twenty pages of a novel and twenty pages of a technical book require completely different levels of effort and focus.

So I started looking for a more universal way to measure progress.

The answer was time.

Time works across almost everything. Reading, learning a language, exercising, building a side project, studying, or simply spending time reflecting. If you're consistently investing time into something meaningful, you're moving forward.

That idea eventually became this project.

Its purpose is not to make people work harder or achieve more in less time.

Its purpose is much simpler.

> I wanted to create a tool that helps people see their own consistency. A tool that reminds them how far they've already come when progress feels invisible.

Because meaningful change rarely happens overnight.

More often, it comes from small actions repeated over long periods of time. And when those actions go unnoticed, they're easy to abandon.

When they become visible, they're much easier to continue.
